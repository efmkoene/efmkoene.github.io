#### Parmenides

Zeno of Elea (hereafter: Zeno) posed a bunch of paradoxes to his contemporaries, a few of which even survived up to the present day. For example, the paradox of Achilles and the Tortoise (Achilles can never catch a tortoise with a head start because every time he reaches the tortoise's previous position, the tortoise has already moved a bit further ahead) is still famous today. 

What is perhaps less well known is that Zeno posed these paradoxes in support of *another* philosopher, named Parmenides. His philosophy rests on two fabulously simple observations: (1) what is, is; (2) what isn't, isn't (that means, "non-being" cannot be thought, referred to, described, or spoken of, because there is literally nothing to think or speak about). A different way to state his philosophy is that it ought to be possible to describe reality ("what is") without ever using the word "not". Once you accept these premises, a number of startling conclusions follow, because change, time, motion, and difference cannot be described without using the word "not", hence, they are not real ("what is")! Change (etc.) are illusory, on Parmenides' account. 

So, when Zeno posed these paradoxes, they were not mere intellectual puzzles. They were to show that Parmenides has it correctly -- change, time, motion, and difference *are* fundamentally impossible, i.e., not real.

#### Zeno's paradox of the arrow

One of the lesser known paradoxes is the paradox of the arrow. It can roughly be stated as follows. Consider an arrow shot by an archer, going from point A at time t₀ to B at time t₂. Now, Zeno asks you: is the arrow moving at any *instant* t₁ in-between t₀ and t₂ (this is like taking a snapshot or photo of the situation at one specific time; an instant is without any duration). Obviously, at a single *instant*, the arrow is not moving -- as the instant doesn't have duration, there is no time for the arrow to travel from one place to another. This holds for any instant -- so Zeno's question ("is the arrow moving at any instant?") has to be answered in the negative ("no, it doesn't move at any instant"). So Zeno can ask a follow-up question: "so.... when does the arrow move?". Again, you come up empty-handed: "there is not *any* instant I can point at when the arrow moves". So, Zeno says, it *never* moves! Sure, it *appears* to move, but in reality, the arrow doesn't (nay -- *cannot*) move. The arrow never actually moves.

#### The modern answer talks past the problem

This paradox was wrestled with for a while in science, and eventually the answer devised in physics was that motion is defined to be the *change of position over time*. This is why looking at a single instant appears not to involve motion, because we should inherently consider more than one instant. Then, using mathematical tools (such as the limit and the derivative), one can even compute the "instantaneous velocity" of the arrow at a single instant. The way this works is to consider two locations of the arrow (their spatial difference is named Δx) at two different times (their temporal difference is named Δt), and computing Δx/Δt gives the average velocity over the time interval Δt. Now, by letting Δt get smaller and smaller (but never all the way to Δt=0!) the average velocity tends to a single limiting velocity, which is named the "instantaneous velocity". Hence, the physicist says: "the arrow *is* moving at the instant t₁, with some speed of X meters per second". Problem solved!

Or is it?

Zeno's point was that motion *appears to happen* (the arrow really does seem to move from point A to B), but then points out that there is no movement at any instant, so motion never *actually* happens. So he inherently splits the world into two -- the apparent movement, and the actual lack of movement. The physicist stands at risk of merely explaining the apparent movement, without actually addressing the actual lack of movement. So let's look closer.

What does the physicist have to say on this paradox? Nothing that touches upon Zeno's point! The physicst *fully agrees with Zeno* that the arrow doesn't move at an instant, which is why they define motion as a change in position *over time*. Secondly, the "instantaneous velocity" is always based on two time instances, however vanishingly small, as Δt is not allowed to ever go to zero. Both cases, thus, imply that the physicst agrees that motion makes no sense at an instant, and can only be made sense of over an interval. Thus, the physicist has not actually challenged Zeno's point at all. They've operationalized a way to quantify motion, to quantify the *apparent* motion of the arrow. But they've admitted defeat to Zeno all the same, it really doesn't move at an instant.

You might think I'm misrepresenting the physcisist's view here, but I'm not. It was even explicitly stated by Bertrand Russel, one of the most important mathematicians and philosophers of the previous century,

> "we live in an unchanging world and ... the arrow, at every moment of its flight, is truly at rest"

In other words, despite all the mathematical tools developed to counter this paradox (and others), we must accept Zeno's original statement of the problem.

#### The conclusion
I think only two possible conclusions follow from this:

1. Zeno (and thus Parmenides) have it right: motion is merely illusory, not actually happening
2. Instants aren't real (or, equivalently, time is not composed of instants)

As I believe the first conclusion (that Zeno and Parmenides have it right) is untenable, let's consider the second option in some  more detail. To go from accepting that "the arrow doesn't move at any *instant*" to accepting that "it doesn't move at any *time*", we need to assume that time is composed of (or can be reconstructed out of) instants. For Zeno's claim to hold, in other words, we need to assume that by summing up an (infinite number of) instants, we obtain a continuous time interval. But this is not straightforwardly true, even in mathematics -- an instant has no duration, so summing up infinity times 0 we still obtain 0.  (This is a different operation from integration, where the "width" of each slice is not literally zero — it is infinitesimally small and is scaled by a corresponding infinitesimal, so the product is finite. Zeno's instants, by contrast, have zero duration, full stop.) In other words, time is *not* composed of instants; or put in yet other words, we can derive the concept of "the instants" from time, but we cannot derive "time" from the instant. Time is real, the instant is an abstraction that doesn't exist in the furniture of reality "as it (really) is". 

Sure, Zeno is correct: the arrow doesn't move at an instant. But as instants aren't real, *this is not an issue*!

In fact, the physicist *has* to assume the same. To compute the instantaneous velocity, which is the limit of Δx/Δt as Δt tends to (but never becomes!) 0, the "instant" has to be part of a "time interval" over which we can take the limit. Thus, also for the physicist, the time interval has to exist *before* "the instant", i.e., it is abstracted out of the time interval.

Zeno assumed that time is composed of indivisible instants in the same way a line might be said to be composed of points. But this is precisely backwards. Henri Bergson argued the same in his book *Creative Evolution*: once reality is reduced to a sequence of immobilities, genuine movement can never reappear, no matter how many frames we append together. The instant is not a constituent of time but an abstraction extracted from an already existing temporal continuum. And as our world contains plenty of motion, I think, it shows that the time in which we live, is not that of an infinite number of instants stitched together.

No matter how many mindfulness teachers tell us to stay in the "now", instant after instant, we have to acknowledge that the "now" might not quite be what we think it is -- certainly not a mere sequence of instants, as instants don't exist! Time is much stranger than we think it is.

> What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one who asks, I know not.

(St. Augustine, around 400 AD)
